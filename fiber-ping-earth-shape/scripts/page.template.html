An Experimental Inquiry

On the Time of Light-Signals in the Telegraph Fibres, and What It Reveals of the Figure of the Earth

Communicated from rooster.ninja · Observations of 12–13 November 2026
❦ ❦ ❦

Abstract

§ I.Of the Principle

No message can outrun light. Whatever carries a signal, glass fibre, copper, radio, laser or any means not yet known to us, it cannot cross a distance faster than light crosses it in a vacuum. Routers, queues and the winding of cables can only add time to the journey; nothing on the path can take time away. Hence the shortest round trip ever measured between two places sets a ceiling on how far apart they can be, and any proposed map of the Earth that puts them farther apart than that ceiling must be wrong.

For a pair of stations separated by a distance d, the round-trip floor set by light in a vacuum is

tvac = 2d / c(1)

This floor assumes nothing about the medium, and it is the one every verdict in this paper rests on. Ordinary telegraph fibre carries light at about two-thirds of that speed (group index ng ≈ 1.462–1.468), giving the stricter floor tfib = 2d ng/c. It is shown throughout for reference only, since we do not presume what carries the signals.

We compute d twice for every pair, each time as the shortest path the model allows. On the globe it is the great-circle arc along the surface of a sphere of 6,371 km. On the flat map, the north-pole azimuthal equidistant projection, a place at latitude φ lies at radius r = (90° − φ) × 111.195 km from the pole, and d is the straight line between the two points:

dflat2 = r12 + r22 − 2r1r2 cos Δλ(2)

No path on a plane can be shorter than that straight line, so the flat-map floor is the most favourable that map can claim. A measured minimum falling below a model’s speed-of-light floor rules that model out for that pair, however the signal was carried. A minimum above both floors decides nothing, for slow routes are allowed under either figure.

§ II.Of the Design of the Experiment, and the Reasons for Each Choice

  1. Minimum, not mean. Every delay on the path is positive, so the fastest of sixteen echoes is the one closest to the physical floor. The mean measures congestion; the minimum measures geometry.
  2. Single-site target servers. Each target is a Vultr test host in one named datacentre. Large services answer from many places at once under one address (“anycast”), which would let a nearby machine reply for a distant city and fake a short time. A single-site host removes that trap.
  3. Three probes on different networks. Each source city pings each target from up to three Globalping probes run by different internet providers. Each provider takes its own route, and only the shortest route tests the floor, so the lowest of the three is kept.
  4. A traceroute from the same probes. The hop list shows where the path really went, through a direct cable or round by Los Angeles and London, and confirms that the last hop is the labelled datacentre.
  5. A “home check” on every server’s position. Each target is also pinged from probes in its own city. A minimum under 6 ms places it within a few hundred kilometres of that city. At such short range the globe and the flat map agree to well under one part in a hundred, so this check fixes the server’s position without assuming either figure of the Earth. Locating servers from long-distance pings would be circular, since turning a time into a distance already presumes a map. § VIII goes further and presumes no position at all.
  6. The speed of light in a vacuum as the floor for both figures. The signals almost certainly travel in glass: none of the probes sits on a satellite network, and the traceroutes show ordinary cable carriers. But an argument resting on the medium can always be answered with an unknown medium. The vacuum floor cannot: no carrier of any kind, known or secret, beats it. Both figures are therefore judged against it alone. The fibre floor is printed beside it as a reference, so a reader can see which results would change if glass were assumed.
  7. A northern source first, a southern source second. From Vancouver the flat map stretches distances by only 0–14 %, less than ordinary route slack, so this series is expected to decide nothing and serves as the calibration. From Santiago the flat map stretches distances by up to two and a third times, and the two figures part company.
  8. Clocks only at the sender. The round trip is timed by one clock at the probe, so its absolute setting cancels. Only clock error can make a reading too fast, and that is fixed at 0.05 ms (§ III). A companion pinger that never puts the time inside the packet, and authenticates every reply with a secret-keyed hash, was used on the stations of our own in § VII.

§ III.Of the Errors

Two errors enter the verdicts. Position: probes and datacentres are known to city level, taken as 25 km (1σ) at each end. This was carried through equations (1) and (2) by 4,000 Monte Carlo trials per pair, giving a vacuum-floor uncertainty of 0.23–0.62 ms, the largest belonging to the far-southern pairs, where the flat map spreads a small error of position over the most ground. Timing: 0.05 ms on the fast side, the only side that matters, since path delays can only lengthen a ping. The group index of glass does not enter the verdicts at all.

The headroom of a pair is its minimum ping less the floor, and its significance is that headroom divided by the combined uncertainty, σ = √(σfloor2 + 0.052). A negative headroom of several σ rules the model out for that pair. The error bars are drawn at ±1σ (heavy) and ±3σ (light), true to scale. On the full-scale plates they are barely wider than the marks; Plate V shows the closest cases on an axis narrow enough to read them. Their smallness is itself a finding: a city-level position error cannot move any floor by more than about two-thirds of a millisecond.

§ VI.Of the Nearest Approaches

The pairs of the two series that come closest to either floor deserve the closest look, for here alone the error bars could matter. Plate V draws them at true scale on a narrow axis. None comes within ten standard errors of a speed-of-light floor; the mesh of § VII will find one that does.

§ VII.Of a Mesh Between Stations of Our Own

Every test so far rests on someone else’s apparatus: probes run by volunteers, and servers whose replies we could not seal. For this last series we rented our own. Every server pinged every other thirty times with the companion pinger of § II.VIII, which stamps each echo with the kernel’s clock at the sender and accepts a reply only if its random, hash-sealed payload comes back intact.

  1. Two providers, not one. Each company buys its own cable capacity and routes its own traffic. Where both have a datacentre in a city, the pair is measured twice over independent networks; a route that ran oddly fast through one would have to be matched by the other.
  2. Every city to every other. The campaigns of §§ IV–V looked out from one city at a time. A mesh measures all 171 pairs at once, including the short southern pairs such as Melbourne–Sydney, where the flat map stretches distance most and the routes are most direct.
  3. A home check that needs no map. Where both providers have a datacentre in the same city, the two servers pinged each other. Light in a vacuum covers no more than half the round trip each way, so that time alone bounds how far apart the two can stand, whatever the figure of the Earth (Table IV).
  4. Proven replies. Every echo counted carried a fresh random payload sealed with a secret key, so no router, cache or impostor could have answered early on the far server’s behalf.

§ VIII.Of Placing Each Server Without Presuming Where It Stands

The tests above take the position of every server as known. That position was itself fixed by the home check, at a range where the two figures agree; but a sceptic may still ask that nothing at all be presumed. This section presumes nothing about where any server stands.

Each of eleven servers was pinged from probes in twenty-four landmark cities spread over both hemispheres, and every one but Santiago, which had no server of ours, was also pinged by fourteen to sixteen of the mesh servers of § VII: thirty to forty-seven landmarks for each server. Every landmark’s fastest echo sets a greatest distance: light in a vacuum can carry a signal no farther, out and back, in that time. Around each landmark we draw a circle of that radius, once on the globe and once on the flat map, and ask a single question of each figure: is there any spot, anywhere, that lies inside every circle at once? If there is, the figure can place the server. If there is none, it cannot place the server anywhere at all, whatever its true position and whatever carried the signal.

The answer is computed as the slack: the largest margin by which any one position can satisfy every circle together. A positive slack means a position exists; a negative slack means none does, short by that many kilometres. Each landmark’s position was varied by 25 km (1σ) through two hundred Monte Carlo trials to give the error.

One caution belongs with this result. The test can only reject. Every circle is a greatest distance, and the north-pole flat map never makes any distance shorter than the globe does, so wherever the flat map finds a position, the globe finds one too. Run on data made up from a flat world, the same method would still let the globe place every server. The test therefore removes the presumption of position and finds the flat map unable to place Sydney or Melbourne at any speed up to that of light; it does not, by itself, single out the globe. That is why the pair-by-pair tests of §§ IV–VII, which bear on both figures alike, stand beside it.

§ IX.Conclusions

Three limits remain, besides the one-sidedness of § VIII. Only one flat layout, the north-pole azimuthal equidistant projection, is tested; other flat arrangements would need their own distance formula. Landmark positions are those the probe network reports, and the mesh servers are placed at their city centres, both known to city level; that matters most for Melbourne–Sydney, which datacentre street addresses would sharpen. And the southern pairs that would decide most strongly by themselves, Santiago to Sydney, Melbourne and Johannesburg, will decide nothing until a route runs direct.

§ X.Of Repeating the Experiment

The apparatus is a set of Python scripts using only the standard library, kept with the raw data in the repository for this page, whose README gives instructions. coastal_campaign.py runs a whole series from any computer through the Globalping network, choosing the source city on the command line. landmark_campaign.py and multilaterate.py collect and analyse the landmark pings of § VIII. verified_ping.py is the companion pinger for a station of one’s own (Linux, run as root); it times each echo from kernel timestamps at the sender, and accepts a reply only if its random, hash-sealed payload returns intact. ping_mesh.py rents the servers of § VII from Vultr and Amazon, runs the mesh, gathers the results and deletes every server afterwards.

python3 coastal_campaign.py Santiago CL
