3 · What the theorem allows

Gauss, 1827

"If a curved surface is developed upon any other surface whatever, the measure of curvature in each point remains unchanged."Disquisitiones generales, Art. 12 (Theorema Egregium)

The proof (Art. 11) gives the measure of curvature by a formula "which involves only the quantities E, F, G and their differential quotients": lengths measured on the surface. That is the whole theorem. It names no triangle, no angle sum and no excess.

The angle-sum theorem is a different one, Art. 20, and Gauss states it through the normals: the excess "is measured by the area of the part of the sphere which corresponds, through the directions of the normals, to that triangle". Getting from excess to curvature exits the Theorema Egregium: it leaves Art. 12 for Art. 20.

Tarski, 1948 / 1951

"A decision method by which the truth of sentences of the elementary algebra and geometry of real numbers is determined."A Decision Method for Elementary Algebra and Geometry, RAND R-109, summary

Plane geometry written in first-order terms (points, betweenness, equal distances) is complete: every sentence in it is mechanically proved or refuted. For survey lengths two sentences matter, and both are decided here for every case:

  1. Triangle. Three lengths form a plane triangle if and only if each is shorter than the sum of the other two. Its plane angles then sum to exactly 180°.
  2. Closed fan. Triangles ringed round one station lie flat if and only if their plane angles at that station sum to exactly 360°.

Inside the Theorema Egregium, curvature must come from lengths. Tarski's method says what lengths alone decide in the plane. Angles, plumb lines and the excess are outside both.

6 · Closed fans: the one lengths-only test, and what it finds

A strip of triangles always lays flat. Curvature can show in lengths only where triangles close around a station: then their plane angles at the centre, computed from sides alone, must total 360° on a flat surface. Schott built both chains of "quadrilaterals or central figures" (SP4 p. 25), so such fans exist: 62 found.

But the deficit equals one third of the fan's printed excess to +0.05″ ± 0.31″, closer than it matches the observed excess (+0.45″ ± 0.76″). The curvature in the lengths is the ellipsoid excess Schott subtracted from each angle to compute the sides. It entered through steps 1–6 of panel 4; it was not measured as a length.

So the fans are an echo, not a test. At face value they read curved, but they only repeat what the angles said, and the angles exit the Theorema Egregium. With these lengths the fans add no information of their own: as a lengths-only test they cannot tell flat from curved.

7 · The measured lengths, and how they were compared

The 39th-parallel chain "is supported by 10 base lines suitably distributed" (SP4 p. 25), measured with bars on the ground. Adjacent bases were compared through the triangulation between them, and Schott says how: the length equation "has been derived from the angles", with "Spherical angles … the logarithms of the terminal lines having been corrected for difference in arc and sine" (SP4 p. 613).

8 · Verdict

Gauss reading. All 220 curved triangles (>3σ), and all 716 tested, owe their curvature to angular excess: plumb-levelled angles, sight lines taken as shortest lines, and Art. 20 rather than Art. 12. Each one exits the Theorema Egregium. They are inductive findings from the printed angles at the stated noise, but not findings of that theorem.

Tarski reading. By lengths, all 746 triangles are flat triangles. The 62 closed fans do refute flatness by lengths, yet only by the excess that Legendre's theorem put into the sides. In this data no length was measured independently of the angles, apart from 3 base sides, and those close nothing by themselves. So the fans add no information of their own: by lengths alone, this data cannot tell flat from curved.

What would stay inside the theorem. A closed fan, or a quadrilateral with both diagonals, whose every side is a measured length. Then the deficit is computed from lengths alone:

  1. Deficit 0 within its error bars: flat.
  2. Deficit above its error bars: curved.
  3. Either way the answer comes from lengths alone. Only turning a nonzero deficit into a value of K needs a further theorem.
9 · Methods
  1. Data. Schott's printed angles, spherical excess and sides for every triangle of the 39th-parallel arc (SP4, 465 triangles) and the Eastern Oblique arc (SP7, 281), as transcribed in the Theorema Egregium report's master KML. Sides are printed as log metres. Observed excess and qc (3 degree misprints corrected from the printed sides) come from the report's triangles_rule.csv.
  2. Gauss reading. ε = sum of the three station-adjusted angles − 180°; z = ε / σ with the report's per-survey closure noise. Triangles with an angle printed "…." are not tested.
  3. Tarski reading. From the three printed sides only: triangle inequality; plane angles by the law of cosines; their sum. Legendre residual = observed angle − printed ε/3 − plane angle, rms over the three corners.
  4. Closed fans. For each station, its neighbours are ordered by map bearing and the finest ring of triangles that wraps 360° without overlap is chosen; each triangle's plane angle at the centre must match its bearing gap within 2°. Deficit = 360° − sum of plane angles at the centre from printed sides.
  5. Measured sides. A side is a measured base when both ends are terminals of the same base line (for example "Dauphin Island East Base" and "Dauphin Island West Base").
