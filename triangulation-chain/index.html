A baseline, seven triangles and a check base

No radius goes in. Each triangle's measured excess (angle sum − 180°) is divided by its area, computed from the measured baseline and the angles, giving K and then R. The last two columns compare that R with an assumed Earth radius of 6,371 km. Rows fill in as each triangle closes.

The combined row is a least-squares fit of one K to all closed triangles: K̂ = Σ(excessᵢ · Aᵢ) / Σ Aᵢ², with σK = σsum / √(Σ Aᵢ²). Small triangles carry almost no weight because their excess is buried in the 1.2″ angle-sum error.

4 · Malice: how hard is it to fudge the numbers?

Pick a data set and watch which checks fire. The honest set is the simulation above. The two forgeries are the obvious ways to cook a triangulation: strip the excess so every triangle closes on 180° (a flat world), or smooth the noise so the closures look too good.

CheckResultWhat it catches

What a convincing forgery needs. Every angle in every triangle must be consistent with one set of station positions on one surface, because a network has more observations than unknowns: each closed triangle, each diagonal across a quadrilateral, and each side computed two ways is a condition the numbers must satisfy at once. Faking that means computing the true geometry first, so the forger needs the answer to fake the data. The scatter must also look like real error: closures near 1″ for first order, readings that vary between the 16 sets as the instruments really did, and no favoured final digits.

Nudge one small triangleEasyUsually nothing: its excess (under 1″) is below the noise. Neighbouring triangles sharing its sides may disagree.

Add excess to a flat world (globe)Equally easy to doThe mirror image: closures against flat scale with area. Angles alone can't say which party fudged; an independent re-measurement can.
Smooth the scatterEasyClosure scatter far below the instrument's known error (χ² per triangle near 0). Raw field books expose it: Méchain concealed a Barcelona latitude discrepancy, and Delambre found it in his notebooks after his death.
A consistent fake networkHardRequires solving the network first. Still exposed by independent checks: other teams' bases, adjoining national networks at their junctions, star latitudes, and modern GNSS re-occupation of surviving monuments.

Independent checks. The check base, measured by a different crew with bars, tests the scale. Junctions with neighbouring surveys (France–England in 1787, the Struve Arc across ten countries) test everything at once, because each side was measured by people with no access to the other's numbers. Surviving monuments can be re-measured today with GNSS: a recent RTK check of one recovered Yolo base stone put it 3 cm in distance from its 1881 record.

5 · Methods

Were all three angles measured? Yes. In primary (first-order) triangulation every angle of every triangle is observed at its own station. The third angle is never computed from the other two, because the closure (sum − 180° − excess) is the triangle's own error check.

How is distance determined? Only the baselines are measured. Bars or tapes are laid end to end along a prepared line, corrected for temperature, slope and sag, then reduced to sea level. Every other side is computed: from one known side and the adjusted angles, by the law of sines, triangle after triangle.

  1. Baseline. 10 km measured with 5 m bars, about 2,000 placements, to 1 part in 300,000 (±33 mm).
  2. Angles. At each station the theodolite is levelled, the circle read on one target, then swung to the next and read again. 16 positions are averaged.
  3. Excess. From the area of each triangle:
E = A / R²,    closure = (α + β + γ − 180°) − E
  1. Legendre's theorem. Subtract a third of the measured (α + β + γ − 180°) from each angle, then solve as a plane triangle. This needs no radius: the excess and the closure are removed together.
b = a · sin β′ / sin α′
  1. Check base. A second base at the far end is measured by bars and compared with the length carried through all seven triangles.
