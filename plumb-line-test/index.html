Field experiment · null-hypothesis design · Alberta, ~50° N

Plumb Line Curvature Test

If the ground is flat, plumb lines at two distant spots point the same way. If the ground curves, they tilt apart. We measure that tilt with the stars straight overhead and the distance with a radio time-of-flight link, then test whether the tilt is zero.

The question

Hypotheses, stated before any data

H₀ · NULL · FLAT κ = 0

Plumb lines at every station are parallel. The angle between zenith directions is zero at any distance.

H₁ · ALTERNATIVE · CURVED (CONVEX) κ > 0

Plumb lines converge below the ground. The zenith angle grows with distance: γ = d / R. Globe predicts R ≈ 6,373 km north–south, 6,391 km east–west at 50° N.

κ is the curvature along one line (radians per km). Two perpendicular legs give the surface's Gaussian curvature, K = κNS × κEW. The sign of κ is measurable too: a concave “bowl” gives κ < 0, a saddle gives opposite signs on the two legs.

How it works

A plumb line points “down” wherever you stand. The opposite direction is your zenith, the point straight overhead. A camera that knows which way is level can photograph the stars and work out exactly which part of the sky is at its zenith.

Stars are a shared, fixed reference. They are so far away that their light arrives parallel at both stations. Any difference between the two zenith points comes from the plumb lines themselves pointing different ways.

The radio link gives the distance. Alpha and Chimp time a signal between the two stations. Light speed times travel time, corrected for air, gives d without relying on maps or GPS models.

Divide. Curvature along the line is κ = γ / d. Flat predicts zero. A globe predicts 1 / R.

Why look straight up instead of at the horizon? Horizon and mountain sightlines pass through tens of kilometres of low air that bends light by an unknown amount (refraction coefficient k). At k = 1 a globe looks perfectly flat. Overhead, starlight passes through the least air and refraction is near zero, so k drops out of the test.

Field procedure

Steps

  1. Pre-register

    Write down H₀, H₁, the threshold and the analysis code, and date them, before any field work (decision rule below).

  2. Mark the stations

    Pick A and B roughly north–south, as far apart as radio line of sight allows. Use masts, terrain, or chained hops for long legs.

  3. Range the leg

    Run Alpha↔Chimp between the marks with weather logging at both ends. Repeat on several days and average.

  4. Zenith session at A

    Rough-level the base. Take 20–30 frames, rotate 180°, take 20–30 more. Log tilt and time for every frame. Repeat on a second clear night.

  5. Zenith session at B (and C)

    Same rig, same procedure. Different nights are fine.

  6. Blind hand-off

    A helper relabels the station files with codes and holds the TOF distances until the zenith reduction is finished.

  7. Reduce

    Plate-solve each frame, apply the tilt correction, and average the reversal pairs to get each station's zenith direction. Compute γ, unblind d, then compute κ and the test statistic.

Analysis

Math and decision rule

γ = angle between zenith directions (from plate-solved star fields)
κ̂ = γ / d    (signed: + if B's zenith tilts toward B's direction of travel)
σκ = √(σA² + σB²) / d   (TOF error is negligible)
z = κ̂ / σκ
K = κ̂NS × κ̂EW   (with the east leg)

Pre-registered decision rule

  • Reject H₀ (flat) if z > 3.3 on a leg (one-sided, α = 0.001).
  • Consistent with globe if |κ̂ − 1/R| < 2σκ, with R = 6,373 km (N–S) or 6,391 km (E–W).
  • Fail to reject H₀ if z < 3.3. Report the upper bound on κ.
  • Star-distance check: γ must agree across different stars and hours. If it varies, the “stars at infinity” assumption fails and the result is void.

Why this design

What this test avoids

No refraction model. The horizon, mountain-peak and laser tests all depend on k, the bending of light in low air, which varies from below 0 to above 1. This test only looks straight up.

No map or GPS geometry. GPS coordinates are computed on an ellipsoid, so using them to “find” curvature is circular. Here the distance comes from radio timing and the angle from the stars.

Both outcomes are possible. The flat model makes a sharp prediction (γ = 0) that this data can confirm or reject. A bowl or saddle would show up as a sign or a mismatch between the legs.

Globe values use the WGS-84 radii of curvature at 50° N. One arcsecond (″) = 1/3600 of a degree. Curvature in the diagrams is exaggerated for visibility.
