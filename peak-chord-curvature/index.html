Theorema Egregium · line-of-sight curvature

Two peaks, an observer and a fourth station

Every distance is one straight Giga-Ranger shot: Nanton to Mount Burke, Nanton to Plateau Mountain, and peak to peak. Those three chords fit any surface. One more station, ranging to all three corners over real terrain, turns them into a radius of curvature with error bars.

4 · Why three chords are not enough

Counting. On a surface, three stations have three unknowns once the surface's own freedom (slide and turn) is removed. Three chords fill them exactly, for a flat surface or a globe of any size. Nothing is left over to test.

A fourth station adds three chords but only two unknowns. Four stations have five unknowns, and six chords measure them. The one extra measurement is a condition that only the right curvature satisfies. For four points on a surface of curvature K, with V the matrix of their pairwise distances run through VK:

1ᵀ V⁻¹ 1 = K,    Vij = VK(sij) = (1 − cos √K sij) / K

Chords need heights. A chord is a straight line through the air, not a distance along the ground. Converting it needs each station's height above the reference surface, measured independently, for example by simultaneous barometric levelling with the BME280s already in each enclosure. On a sphere of signed curvature u = 1/R (u > 0 convex, 0 flat, u < 0 concave):

c² = (h₁ − h₂)² + 2 (1 + u h₁)(1 + u h₂) · Vu²(s)

Flat or curved distance? Both, by design. The conversion from chord to ground distance depends on the shape being tested: at u = 0 it is the flat result, s = √(c² − Δh²); at u = 1/R it is the arc along a sphere of radius R. The fit tries each u and keeps the one where the four distances close. Choosing one conversion in advance would assume the answer, and the difference between the two conversions is part of what the test reads.

Where the station goes matters, and terrain decides. On paper the best fourth station sits inside the triangle, in the foothills near 50.28° N, 114.28° W, which would put flat about 17σ away. The terrain check says no site there can see all three corners: foothill ridges block at least one chord, even with 30 m masts. The usable sites are on the prairie just east of Nanton. With 15 m masts at both ground stations, a station near 50.365° N, 113.64° W sees all three corners with the Fresnel zone clear and puts flat about 8σ away; 30 m masts near 50.37° N, 113.60° W give about 10σ. Outside both zones (High River, for example) the test drops below 1σ.

What the test reads. The fit uses how the chords sit against the station heights as well as the shape of the four-point figure. With all four stations at the same height the test still separates flat from the globe (about 3σ here), from the line-to-line difference between chord and ground. The 1.5 km height difference between the peaks and the prairie multiplies that about six-fold.

5 · Methods
  1. Chords. Range every pair of the four stations, 500 exchanges per pair, corrected for calibration and the GRIN air delay.
  2. Heights. Read pressure, temperature and humidity at all four stations at the same time and level them barometrically relative to one another. Only differences matter: a shift of all four heights together cancels.
  3. Fit. For a trial u, convert each chord to a ground distance with the heights, then test the four-point condition. Solve for the u that satisfies it. R = 1/u.
  4. Line of sight. Check every chord against the terrain before choosing sites; the map does this live.
  5. Error bars. Repeat the campaign 1,500 times with fresh simulated errors; the scatter of û is its 1σ.

No angles, bearings or GPS. Coordinates here only build the simulated world and the map.
