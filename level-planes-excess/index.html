Of level planes and plumb lines

Three level planes and the angle excess

In brief.
  • Every reading is Euclidean: straight sight lines, flat level planes, no arc or sphere in any measurement.
  • The excess comes from one input: the directions given to the verticals. Parallel, it vanishes; meeting at a point, it appears. A flat pyramid gives the same readings.
  • It fixes only a total, equal to the apex angle defect, and cannot say where the turning lies.
  • K adds nothing: it divides the excess by an area that was never measured.
  • Computation is not measurement: the converging verticals are assumed, not observed, and the survey excess is neither intrinsic nor a measurement of the verticals.
  • So the closure is not evidence of a sphere. The verticals must be measured directly: Appendix A.

§ II. What each theodolite reads

Each dial is the theodolite's horizontal circle, seen along its vertical axis. The two sight lines are projected onto the circle's plane, which is all a theodolite can do, and the angle between them is read off. Nothing else goes in. Normally the axis is the station's own plumb line; with the plumbs corrected it is tipped off plumb by the amount shown.

§ IV. Side view along one side: the verticals converge

The angle between the two stations' horizontal planes equals the angle between their verticals. Each sight line drops below its own station's horizontal by half that angle. On the plane (R = ∞) the verticals are parallel, both horizontals are the same plane, and the dip is zero.

face angles at the apex:  αA + αB + αC = 360° − E    (angle defect = excess)

The angle defect at the apex, 360° minus the three face angles that meet there, comes out equal to the triangle's angle excess. On the flat-faced pyramid all of the triangle's curvature sits at that one point. This is Descartes' angle defect, the polyhedral form of Gauss's result: the face normals are the three plumb lines, and the defect is the area of the triangle they span on the unit sphere. Check with the octant preset: the planes form a cube corner, 90° + 90° + 90° = 270°, a defect of 90°.

The highlighted pieces are the triangle as the three level planes see it: each runs from its station along the two sight lines to the ridges over the side midpoints, and up the ridges to the apex. Each is a flat kite with right angles where it meets the ridges, so its area is exact:

piece = R² · tan(s/2R) · √( tan²ρ − tan²(s/2R) ),    sin ρ = (2/√3) sin(s/2R)

Three pieces always come out a little larger than the sphere's triangle, and the flat triangle through A, B and C a little smaller. On the plane all three are equal.

Where is the sphere?

Nowhere in the measurement. Every quantity a theodolite touches on this page is a straight line, a flat plane, or an angle between two lines: plain Euclidean geometry in three dimensions. The pyramid gives the same readings with nothing round in it.

The assumptions put in by hand. None of these is measured by the triangle; each is supplied before the first angle is read.

  1. A common centre. The plumb lines of A, B and C, produced downward, are made to meet in a single point.
  2. Equal distance. Every station is placed at the same distance R from that point.
  3. The same everywhere. The convergence is taken to be equal in every direction and at every place, so that any other station would point at the same centre.
  4. The ground between. The surface between stations is taken to follow the same rule, though no instrument stands there. The pyramid shows it need not.
  5. The vertical is the plumb line. Each instrument's axis is taken to be that line exactly, with nothing local pulling it aside.

Grant the first alone, for these three stations only, and the excess follows. The remaining four are what the word sphere adds, and a single triangle is silent on all of them.

So the sphere is an assumption about the verticals, not a finding. What would make it a finding is itself a matter of straight lines: produce the plumb lines of many stations downward and see whether they all pass through one point, at one distance from every station. Three lines in space generally miss one another, so their meeting is a real test; three stations are too few to tell a sphere from this pyramid, and many stations are needed. Arcs and spherical trigonometry are a convenience for working on a surface already assumed, never the evidence for it.

§ VI. Excess against area, for any radius

Every point is measured the same way as the readouts: angles from the dials, area from tiling. On log axes each sphere gives a straight line of slope 1, meaning the excess is proportional to area, and the line's height is K. Lines move down as R grows. At R = ∞ the excess is zero for every area, and zero has no place on a log axis.

§ VII. What the excess is measuring

The surface between stations never enters. A theodolite only knows two things: the direction of its own vertical, which sets its horizontal plane, and the directions of the sight lines to the other stations. Draw the same three stations with parallel verticals and the angles add up to exactly 180°, whatever the ground in between is doing.

The excess comes from the verticals not being parallel. Tilt the three planes against each other and the projected angles no longer close to 180°. Gauss showed the excess equals the area that the triangle's normals sweep out on a unit sphere (his "total curvature", Disquisitiones generales, Art. 20). For a sphere of radius R that area is A / R².

Correcting the plumbs to one horizontal plane. The toggle tips every station's zenith axis toward the triangle's centre by Δ, the station's angular distance from the centre (Δ = d / R for a distance d from the centre; 32.4″ per km on Earth). Nothing is subtracted from any reading: each instrument is turned in 3D, so its horizontal circle turns in a different plane and the projections change. All three horizontals become one horizontal plane through A, B and C, every sight line runs along it with zero dip, and the sum is exactly 180° on any sphere.

The correction needs the curvature it removes. To set Δ you must know, at every station, how its plumb line points relative to the others' (the angle between plumb lines in the readouts). That is the convergence of the verticals, which is the curvature. On a plane, Δ = 0 at every station. A level bubble can't supply it, because a bubble only finds the local plumb line. It has to come from outside the triangle, such as stars, or from the excess itself.

What this page assumes. Here the verticals are the sphere's normals by construction, so the page shows what a perfect sphere would give. It does not show that Earth is one. On Earth the vertical is set by gravity, so a real survey measures the shape of the level surface, and any deflection of the vertical feeds straight into the angles.

§ VIII. Methods
  1. Stations. An equilateral triangle with sides s measured along the surface. On a sphere the three stations sit on a circle of angular radius ρ around the centroid:
sin ρ = (2/√3) · sin(s / 2R)
  1. Verticals and level planes. At station i the vertical is the unit normal ui. The level plane is spanned by two tangent vectors e1, e2 square to it.
  2. Angles, as a theodolite reads them. Each sight line to station j is projected onto the plane, d = (uj·e1, uj·e2). The angle is the angle between the two projected directions:
α = atan2( dj × dk, dj · dk )
  1. Area, without the angles. The triangle is cut into 9,216 small flat facets whose corners lie on the surface. Their areas are summed, and the result is repeated at 2,304 facets and extrapolated (Richardson) to the true surface area.
  2. Corrected plumbs. With the plumbs corrected, each instrument's frame (axis n, plane basis e1, e2) is replaced by the centroid's vertical and its horizontal. Sight lines are the chords uj − ui projected onto that plane. Correction Δ = angle between ui and n, which equals ρ; dip = asin(chord · n / |chord|).
  3. Curvature. K = E / A from the two measured quantities. The page never uses E = A / R²; agreement with 1 / R² is a check, shown in the readouts. On the plane, everything is the same calculation with parallel verticals.
  4. Precision. All arithmetic is double precision and arranged to avoid cancellation, so excesses down to about 10⁻¹⁴ rad (2 × 10⁻⁹″) are reliable. Below that the check column shows rounding noise.

Conclusion.
  1. Every reading is Euclidean. Sight lines are straight, level planes are flat, and each angle is two straight lines projected onto one plane. No arc, circle or sphere enters any measurement on this page.
  2. The excess comes from one input. It is produced entirely by the directions given to the three verticals. Set them to meet in a point and the excess appears; set them parallel and it vanishes. The flat pyramid of § V gives the same readings with nothing round in it.
  3. The excess fixes only a total. It equals the angle defect at the pyramid's apex exactly. It cannot say where the turning lies, or what shape carries it.
  4. K adds nothing. K = E / A divides a measured angle by an area that is not measured: the area depends on the surface assumed to span the triangle, and the sphere, the pyramid and the flat chord triangle each give a different one. K says nothing the excess does not already say, and its name brings in a surface the instruments never touched.
  5. Computation is not measurement. Triangulation measures flat angles, computes an area, and attributes the excess to converging verticals. The convergence is inferred from that assumption, not observed. A computation from an assumption cannot also be the evidence for it.
  6. The survey excess is not an intrinsic measurement. The Theorema Egregium concerns what can be found from within a surface: the lengths of its own shortest lines and the angles between them, measured along it. A theodolite measures neither. Its angles lie between straight sight lines through the air, read in a plane set by gravity at each instrument. That is the outside route, through the directions of the verticals, which is Gauss's own definition of total curvature as the area the normals sweep out. The theorem says the outside and inside quantities agree for a surface and its own normals; it cannot supply the assumption that gravity's verticals are the normals of the surface under the instruments. The survey excess is therefore not a measurement of the surface. It is not a measurement of the verticals either: they are never observed in the survey, only assumed, and the excess is computed from that assumption.

None of this shows that the verticals do not converge. It shows that the triangle closure cannot be the evidence that they do. That question needs the verticals themselves to be measured: Appendix A.

Appendix A. Measuring the verticals directly

To measure the angle between two stations' plumb lines, each plumb line has to be compared with something both stations share. Four ways are known; each rests on one assumption of its own, and each assumption can be tested.

MethodHowAssumesHow the assumption is tested
1 · Shared star fieldA zenith camera at each station photographs the stars overhead at the same instant (GPS time). Each frame shows where that plumb line points among the stars; the difference between frames is the angle between the two plumb lines, in both directions.Light from each star arrives parallel at both stations.Every star in the frame must give the same offset. A near source shows different offsets for different stars and directions.
2 · Ranged targetsFlashes, a drone or a balloon in the air. All station-to-target distances measured by time of flight fix every point in space by straight lines alone. Each station then reads the zenith angle to each target from its own plumb line; two or three targets fix each plumb line in that shared frame.Straight light paths to the targets.Near-vertical sightlines keep bending small; more targets than needed must agree with one another.
3 · Reciprocal vertical anglesA and B sight each other at the same instant, and each reads the dip of the line below its own horizontal. The two dips add to the angle between the plumb lines, along that line only.A straight sight line.Weakest of the four: near the ground the line bends by an amount that changes with temperature and time of day. Simultaneous reading cancels part of it.
4 · Carried directionA gyro-stabilised platform is levelled at A, carried to B, and compared with B's level.Gyro drift known to better than the angle sought.Carry the platform out and back: it must return to A's level.

What a direct result is. With method 1 or 2 the angle between two plumb lines is read, and the chord between the stations is ranged. Their ratio, angle per unit of chord, is the convergence, with no area, no excess and no surface.

The test for a common centre. With three or more stations, carry each measured plumb line downward in the ranged frame. Lines in space generally pass one another without meeting, so whether they meet in a single point, at one distance from every station, is a real test. Their closest-approach distances, against the measurement error, are the result. Three stations are the minimum; many are needed to say the same holds everywhere.

What limits it. A zenith camera reads a plumb line to about 0.1″. The plumb line itself is pulled aside by nearby masses, a few arcseconds and up to an arcminute in mountains (§ III), so that pull, not the instrument, sets the limit, and it must be averaged over many station pairs. For method 3, the bending of a sight line near the ground is far from steady: the coefficient Gauss averaged at about 0.13 has been observed to range from −4 to +16 near the ground on sunny days.
