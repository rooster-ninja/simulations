Design review · zenith camera + radio ranging · Alberta, ~50° N

Plumb Line Test Review

What the test measures, what it outputs, and why a positive result on its own does not rule out every flat model.

The test in one paragraph

Two stations, one distance, one angle

Two stations are set up 55 km apart, roughly north–south. A radio time-of-flight link measures the distance between them. At each station, a camera pointed straight up photographs the stars while a tilt sensor records the local vertical. That gives each station's zenith, the point among the stars directly above its plumb line. The angle between the two zeniths, γ, divided by the distance, says how fast “straight up” turns as you travel.

Measurements and outputs

What each part produces

1 · DISTANCE BETWEEN STATIONS (d)
Instrument
Two SX1280 radios (Alpha, Chimp) with 13 dBi Yagis
Method
Round-trip radio timing. d = (c / n) · t, where n is the air's refractive index from pressure, temperature and humidity logged at both ends (correction ≈ 250–300 ppm, about 15 m over 55 km). The straight antenna-to-antenna chord is then converted to a ground distance using the antenna heights.
Output
tof_distance_km, e.g. 55.0213 km, ±~1 m
Assumptions
None about the Earth's shape. No maps, GPS positions or ellipsoid.
2 · ZENITH AT EACH STATION
Instrument
Pi 4, Raspberry Pi HQ camera with 25 mm f/1.8 lens (~13″/pixel, 14° × 11° field), ADXL355 tilt sensor, worm-drive turntable
Method
About 80 frames over 7–8 minutes, turning 0°, 180°, 180°, 0° (twice). Each frame is plate-solved against a star catalog. The tilt sensor gives the plate's tilt from the plumb line. The reversals cancel sensor offset; the ABBA order cancels temperature drift. The result is the zenith position among the stars, written as astronomic latitude (zenith declination) and longitude (zenith right ascension − Greenwich sidereal time).
Output
Per-station summary, ±5–10″

  "astro_lat_deg":  50.337412,  "sigma_lat_arcsec": 4.8,
  "astro_lon_deg": -113.712205, "sigma_lon_arcsec": 5.6,

  "median_fit_rms_arcsec": 1.8, "status": "OK" }

Example values to show the format, not a measurement.

3 · ANGLE BETWEEN THE ZENITHS (γ)
γ ≈ √( Δlat² + (Δlon · cos lat)² )
κ = γ / d   (how fast “up” turns, per km)
R = d / γ   (implied radius, γ in radians)
z = γ / σγ   (distance from zero in error bars)
Predictions
Flat ground, distant stars: γ = 0″
Globe: γ ≈ 1,780″ at 55 km (R ≈ 6,373 km north–south)
Decision rule
Reject “flat” if z > 3.3. Globe-consistent if γ is within 2σ of 1,780″.
Output
Combined result once both stations and the range are in
{ "leg": "A-B", "tof_distance_km": 55.0213,
  "gamma_arcsec": 1772.4, "sigma_gamma_arcsec": 7.4,

Celestial navigation

This is the navigator's rule, re-measured

Celestial navigation rests on one relation: 1 minute of arc of zenith shift = 1 nautical mile of travel. Move 1 NM north and the celestial pole rises 1′.

55 km ÷ 1.852 km/NM = 29.7 NM → 29.7′ ≈ 1,782″

That is the globe prediction above. The plumb line test is cel nav done with a camera and a tilt sensor instead of a sextant and a horizon.

In the navigator's celestial sphere, the observer sits at the centre and stars are placed by altitude and azimuth. The sphere's radius never enters. Cel nav works on directions only, and so does this test.

The critique

Why a positive result does not settle it

The test measures the angle between two plumb lines relative to the star field. That equals the ground's curvature only if the stars are effectively infinitely far away, so both stations see each star in the same direction.

The null hypothesis was written as “flat ground, stars at infinity.” Flat-Earth models usually place the stars on a nearby dome instead. With a dome:

  • Plumb lines are parallel.
  • Each station's vertical meets the dome at a different point, so each sees a different star overhead.
  • The apparent zenith angle is γ ≈ d / H. With H ≈ 6,400 km this gives the same 1,780″ as the globe.
Modelγ predicted at 55 kmRuled out by γ?
Flat ground, distant stars0″yes
Globe, distant stars1,780″ (d / R)no, consistent
Flat ground, star dome at H ≈ d / γ1,780″ (d / H)no, consistent

What a result of γ ≈ 1,780″ establishes

That the overhead star field shifts by 1′ per nautical mile of travel. The globe and a flat-plus-dome model both predict this. The test, as designed, rejects only the strawman flat model with distant stars.

γ is also an extrinsic quantity: how the vertical turns as you move. It is not the intrinsic Gaussian curvature of the ground.

Where the models part ways

Distance is what separates them

On a globe the zenith shift grows in proportion to distance: 1′ per NM everywhere, in every direction. Under a flat star layer at height H, the angle to a star at ground distance x is atan(x / H), which falls behind as x grows. With H = R:

Ground distanceGlobe: x / RFlat + dome: atan(x / H)Difference
55 km (this test)0.4946°0.4946°0.04″
1,000 km8.99°8.92°4.4′ (≈ 4 NM)
3,000 km26.98°25.21°106′ (≈ 106 NM)
5,000 km44.97°38.13°410′ (≈ 410 NM)

The flat column uses the simplest version: a flat star layer at height H directly above the plane. Other dome shapes give other numbers, but every finite-height layer departs from the straight 1′ = 1 NM line over long distances.

At 55 km the difference is 0.04″, far below what any instrument here can see. At ocean-crossing distances it grows to hundreds of nautical miles. Navigators get position fixes that close using the uniform globe relation, worldwide and in both hemispheres. That long-range behaviour is evidence this local test cannot supply by itself.

Status

Open items

  • Star distance. The test needs an independent measurement that the globe and dome models predict differently. To be revisited.
  • Longer baselines. Chained radio hops can reach 150–200 km, which sharpens R. Even at 200 km the globe–dome difference stays small, so on its own this doesn't settle the question.
  • A star-free test. Radio ranging alone, using five stations on a level reference such as a large lake: the centre of a 55 km square sits about 119 m above the plane of its corners on a globe and 0 m on a flat surface.
